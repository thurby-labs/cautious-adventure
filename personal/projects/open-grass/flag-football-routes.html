Flag football routes: the route tree

Ten routes cover almost everything a flag football offense runs. Here’s each one on a single route tree, how deep to run it, and which defenses it beats.

Short routes

Flat 0–5 yds, to the sideline

Release outside and work toward the sideline, catching it about 3–5 yards deep. Quick and easy for young quarterbacks. Best against Cover 3, which leaves the flats empty; tough against Cover 2, where the corners sit in the flats.

Hitch 5 yds, turn back

Push to 5 yards, stop hard and turn back to the quarterback. Beats defenders who play soft and deep, like Cover 3 corners. Against man, the hard stop is the separation.

Out 5 yds, break outside

Run 5 yards, plant and break flat to the sideline. Great against man because the defender has to react to the break. Risky against Cover 2, with a corner waiting in the flat.

Middle routes

Slant 2 steps, then 45° inside

Two hard steps upfield, then cut inside at an angle. The quick break beats man, and against Cover 2 it fits in the hole between the corner and the linebacker. Throw it in front of the receiver, not at them.

In 5 yds, break inside

Run 5 yards and break flat across the field. Finds the soft spot between the corner and linebacker in Cover 2, and the gaps between hook defenders in zone.

Crosser across the field, 6–10 yds

Release, then run across the field, gaining depth. The best man-coverage beater in flag football: defenders have to chase through traffic. Pair it with a route from the other side to create a rub.

Deep routes

Corner 7 yds, break to the sideline

Run 7 yards, then break at an angle toward the back corner. The classic Cover 2 beater: it lands behind the corner and outside the safety. Against Cover 1, run it away from the lone safety.

Post 7 yds, break to the middle

Run 7 yards, then break deep toward the middle. Splits the two safeties in Cover 2. Tough against Cover 1 and Cover 3, which keep a defender in the deep middle.

Go (fade) straight upfield

Run straight and fast. Against man it only wins with a faster receiver, and Cover 3 takes it away. Its real job is often to clear space by pulling a deep defender with it.

Wheel from the slot or backfield

Start toward the flat, then turn up the sideline. Hard for a linebacker to carry in man or Cover 1, and it stretches the flat defender in zone.

Routes work in combinations

One route rarely beats a defense by itself. Good plays pair routes so a single defender has to choose, and the quarterback throws to whoever that defender leaves.

High-low: corner over flat

Two receivers to one side, one deep to the corner and one short to the flat. The defender in between can’t cover both. Open Grass grades this sample play A against Cover 1 and Cover 3, but D against man, where every receiver has a defender.

Coaching routes in 5-on-5

  • Keep it short. Young quarterbacks are most accurate inside 10 yards, so the flat, hitch, slant and out do most of the work.
  • Teach depth with landmarks. “Five big steps, then break” sticks better than yard numbers for young players.
  • Sell the break. A hard plant before the cut is what creates space against man coverage.
  • Give every route a job. A go route that never gets the ball still matters if it pulls a deep defender away from the play.

Questions

What is a route tree in flag football?

A route tree is a diagram of every route a receiver can run from one spot, drawn together so they branch like a tree. It gives your team shared names for each route, so you can call a play in a few words.

What are the best routes for young kids?

Start with the flat, hitch, slant and go. They’re easy to learn, short enough for young quarterbacks to throw, and together they attack the defense short, middle and deep.

What route beats Cover 2?

Corners and posts. Cover 2 has two deep safeties and corners who sit in the flats, so the soft spots are behind the corners near the sideline and in the middle between the safeties.

What route beats man coverage?

Routes with hard breaks (slants, outs) and routes that cross (crossers, slants from both sides). Crossing routes make defenders chase through traffic.
